# Stories and story games

Read this file whenever the learner starts or continues a story, asks for a story game, or does a review session with an active series. The stories are the backbone of the skill. New words arrive in episodes, review happens by asking about the plot, and the games give the learner a reason to come back beyond "keep my streak."

Why stories work: a word tied to a moment in a plot ("the downpour that ruined Marta's date") is far easier to recall than a word on a card. Curiosity about what happens next also pulls people back in a way drills can't.

## 1. Starting a series

When the learner first asks for a story, or you offer one and they accept:

1. Pitch **3 short premises** (2–3 sentences each, written in the explanation language) that fit their interests and goals, and set in a place where the target language is spoken. Use a different genre for each, for example:
   - a small-town mystery (someone keeps leaving notes at the bakery)
   - a telenovela/drama (two rival food trucks, one love triangle)
   - slice of life (the learner's alter ego moves to the city and has to find a flat)
   - adventure or quest (a lost family recipe, a road trip, a treasure map)
   - light sci-fi or fantasy (a café where customers arrive from different decades)
   The learner can also pitch their own.
2. Ask one question: **should the learner be a character?** Having a character called "you" (written in the second person, or with a name they choose) makes chat and role-play blend into the story. Otherwise they're a reader.
3. Create a small cast: **3–4 named characters**, each with one clear trait and a way of speaking (one uses a lot of slang, one is very formal, one talks in proverbs). These voices naturally expose the learner to different registers.
4. Give each season a **central question**, like a mystery to solve, a goal to reach or a choice to make. A season is about 8–12 episodes, and the central question is what the season game is built around (see section 5).
5. Save the series to the story file (section 6) before writing episode 1.

## 2. Writing an episode

Each episode has this structure:

1. **Previously on…:** 1–3 sentences in the target language recapping the last episode. This is where most due review words go (section 3).
2. **The episode:** the new scene, pitched to the learner's level:

   | Level | Length | Style |
   |---|---|---|
   | Pre-A1 | 6–10 short dialogue lines | Every line followed by a translation; romanization if needed |
   | A1 | ~80–120 words | Mostly dialogue, present tense, glosses under the text |
   | A2 | ~150–200 words | Past tenses appear; glosses for new words only |
   | B1 | ~250–350 words | Narration + dialogue, opinions, connectors; glosses on request |
   | B2+ | ~400–600 words | Idioms, humor, register shifts, subtext; no glosses unless asked |

   Aim for about 95% of the text using words the learner already knows (their saved vocab plus words common at their level). Put **3–8 new words** in bold, fewer at lower levels.
3. **One authentic cultural element per episode:** a character says a real proverb, hums a folk song or public-domain poem, cooks a regional dish, or goes to a real festival. Keep it true to the culture where the story is set.
4. **A cliffhanger or open question at the end.** Keep it light, a hook rather than melodrama.
5. **New words:** a compact list with each bolded word, its meaning *in this scene*, and key grammar notes (gender, irregular forms, register).
6. **What next?** Offer the story questions or a game (sections 4–5), and at the very end let the learner shape the plot:
   - A1–A2: choose between 2–3 options written in the target language (understanding the options is part of the practice).
   - B1+: offer options, or ask them to write what the character does next in a sentence or two. Correct gently, and canonize their idea in the next episode.

Save each new word to the profile's vocabulary, with its sentence from the episode as the context sentence and the source recorded as `story S1E3`.

Keep continuity: re-read the story file before writing, reuse names, places and running jokes, and let earlier events matter. Callbacks to old episodes are one of the best review tools there is.

## 3. Weaving review words into the story

Before writing an episode, pull the words due for review from the profile (up to about 8).

- Use each due word **naturally** once in the recap or episode, preferably in a situation that echoes where it was first learned ("It's raining again. Not an *aguacero* like last week, thank goodness").
- **Don't mark review words.** Noticing and understanding them without help is the review.
- Afterwards, check them through story questions (section 4). A word counts as reviewed when the learner answers a question about it or uses it correctly. Grade it got it / shaky / missed and update the schedule as in the main SKILL.md.
- A word that isn't from a story (from chat or a pasted song, say) can still show up in an episode. That's how outside words get pulled into the story world.
- If more words are due than fit naturally, run a short story-question round (section 4) on the older episodes before the new episode.

## 4. Story questions (review)

This is the default way to review when a series is active. Ask **4–8 questions**, one or two at a time, mixing types. Every question must make the learner understand or use a specific target word or structure. Plain plot trivia doesn't count.

| Type | Example | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **What did it mean?** | In «Nos pilló un aguacero», what happened to them? | recognition, A1+ |
| **Fill the quote** | Marta said: «Echo de ___ a mi abuela». What's missing? | recall, A1+ |
| **Who said it?** | Who said «No me gusta madrugar»? Why did they say it? | recognition + comprehension |
| **Spot the lie** | 3 statements about the episode in the target language, one false: which one? | reading comprehension, A2+ |
| **Why / how** | Why was Luis embarrassed at the party? (needs *vergüenza*) | comprehension + production, A2+ |
| **Callback** | Remember the downpour in episode 2? Describe today's weather using that word. | long-term recall |
| **Be the character** | Answer as Marta: your brother keeps pestering you. What do you say? (needs *dar la lata*) | production, B1+ |
| **Retell** | Tell me what happened in 2–3 sentences. | production, A2+ |
| **Predict** | What do you think the note means? Use «a lo mejor». | production, B1+ |

Questions and answers are in the target language as much as the learner's level allows. At Pre-A1/A1, questions can be in the explanation language with target-language quotes.

After the questions, give a short in-story summary ("Marta would be proud: 6 out of 7 clues found"), not a score report.

## 5. Games

Games sit on top of story questions. Keep the stakes playful, and never make the learner feel they failed. A missed word just comes back in a later episode.

### Season mystery (the main game)
- Each season has a central question (who's leaving the notes? where is the recipe?).
- The learner **earns clues** by answering story questions and review items: roughly one clue for every 3 good answers, and a bonus clue for using a new word correctly in their own sentence.
- Clues are short and in the target language ("Clue 4: the handwriting on the note is very small and very neat"), so reading them is practice too. Keep the list of earned clues in the story file.
- At any point, the learner can make a guess **in the target language**, explaining their reasoning with the clues. A wrong guess leads to a plot twist, not a loss. At the end of the season, reveal the answer, celebrate and pitch season 2.

### Spot the lie
Three or four statements about recent episodes, one of them false. It works at every level from A2 up. It's quick, so it makes a good 5-minute snack.

### Choose your path
The end-of-episode choice from section 2, made more game-like. At a turning point, give 2–3 options where picking sensibly requires understanding the words (e.g. one option relies on a false friend). Consequences carry into the next episode.

### Who said it?
Quote lines from characters across several episodes. The learner names the speaker and explains the line. It reinforces how each character talks (slang vs. formal) along with the vocabulary.

### Rewind
The learner retells an episode from one character's point of view. Claude replies as another character who disagrees with their version ("That's not how it happened! I wasn't *embarrassed*, I was...").

Offer a game rather than forcing one. If the learner just wants the next episode, give them the next episode.

## 6. The story file

Keep stories in a separate file next to the profile, `<language>-story.md`, so the profile stays short. Re-read it before every episode.

```markdown
# <Series title>
- Genre: <mystery / telenovela / ...>
- Setting: <city/region, era>
- Learner's role: <reader | character "Name">
- Season: 1 — Central question: <who is leaving notes at the bakery?>
- Episodes so far: 3

## Cast
- Marta — baker, 30s, talks in proverbs
- Luis — her brother, teenager, all slang
- Sra. Ortega — neighbor, very formal, knows everything

## Running threads
- The notes (3 found so far), Luis's secret band, the broken oven

## Clues earned (season game)
1. <clue in target language>

## Episode log
- S1E1 (2026-09-20) — <2–3 line summary> — new: madrugar, la sobremesa, ...
- S1E2 (2026-09-22) — <summary> — new: el aguacero, ... — learner chose: <choice>
```

Record episode summaries, not full texts. Summaries keep the file small and are all you need for continuity and questions. If the learner wants to reread an old episode, rewrite it from the summary and the saved context sentences.

If no filesystem is available, include the story file content in the end-of-session printout, together with the profile.

## 7. Folk tales and one-off stories

- **Folk tales:** retelling a traditional tale of the target culture at the learner's level is great for both culture and language. Examples include Grimm tales for German, Anansi stories for Twi, Nasreddin Hodja for Turkish and the Monkey King for Mandarin. Folk tales that are in the public domain can be retold freely. Use the same episode structure and questions.
- **One-off stories:** a single short story on a topic the learner picks, for a break from the series. Its words are saved with the source `story: <title>`.

## 8. Content rules

- Write original stories. Genres and tropes are fine to borrow. Characters, plots or text from copyrighted books, films or shows are not: offer an original story with a similar feel instead.
- Keep it warm and fun: light humor, stakes that matter to the characters, nothing gratuitously dark unless an adult learner asks for a more mature genre.
- Culture should be accurate and respectful. Avoid stereotypes. Characters from the target culture are ordinary, varied people.
